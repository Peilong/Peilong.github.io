    Exam schedules are a coloring puzzle

    Every college has to fit its final exams into a few time slots so that no student has two exams at once. Computer scientists draw this as a graph: each course is a dot, and a line joins two courses that share a student. Giving every course a slot so that no line joins two courses in the same slot is called graph coloring, because the slots are usually drawn as colors. In 1967 the mathematicians Dominic Welsh and Martin Powell showed how a simple coloring method applies to timetabling, and scheduling software still builds on ideas like theirs.

    Why the fewest slots is hard to find

    Checking a finished schedule is easy: look at every line. Finding one with the fewest slots is another matter. In 1972 Richard Karp showed that this problem belongs to a large family of problems for which nobody knows a fast method, and it is still one of computing’s famous hard problems. With a few hundred courses, trying every schedule is out of reach, so real scheduling software uses clever shortcuts that find good answers, not always the best one.

    Four slots are always enough here

    These puzzles are drawn so that no two lines cross. For any drawing like that, four slots are always enough. This is the Four Color Theorem, first asked about maps in 1852 and proved in 1976 by Kenneth Appel and Wolfgang Haken: the first major theorem proved with the help of a computer. Real exam schedules have lines crossing everywhere, so they can need many more slots.
